tags: [career, writing]

A resume has one clear job: help someone quickly decide whether you might be a fit for a role.

But that also means a lot of real experience gets compressed into a few keywords: technologies, modules, responsibilities, skills.
The details that actually show how someone works often stay outside the resume.

- Why did you build that project?
- How did you make decisions when things went wrong?
- What did you try before finding the right approach?
- What did you learn?

Those answers often reveal more than a list of skills.

A resume still matters. But if your entire job search is just polishing the same document and sending more applications, people may never see the person behind it.

I think a job search can start long before you click “Apply.”

A PR to an open-source project, a technical thought on X or LinkedIn, or a blog post explaining a trade-off can all become ways for people to understand how you think and build.

It doesn’t have to be a deep article every time.

- Solved a problem? Write down the solution.
- Hit a painful bug? Explain the cause.
- Disagree with a technical choice? Share your reasoning.

Small pieces add up. Over time, people can see how you make decisions, communicate, and work.

That’s what personal brand means to me: when someone thinks about a certain kind of problem, they might think of you.

This is also why I built my personal website.

- Projects show what I’ve built.
- My blog records how I think.
- The AI interface gives visitors another way to explore my experience based on what they care about.

Instead of understanding me through a one-page resume, they can explore the work itself.

Of course, a website only matters if there is something real behind it. Good design cannot replace real experience.
My goal is to keep updating it until it becomes a living overview of my work, decisions, experiments, and growth.

Hiring is ultimately a process of mutual understanding.
If someone has already seen your projects or read your writing before an interview, the conversation can move beyond:

> “What do you know?”

and into:

> “Why did you build it this way?”

That is a much better starting point.

So yes, write a strong resume.
But also build in public, contribute, share what you learn, and create a place where people can find your work.

Visibility matters. So does having something worth discovering.

Don’t let years of work become only a few lines on a resume.
